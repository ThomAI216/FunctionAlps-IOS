A habit is a small action tied to a cue you already have, repeated until it runs on its own.

Think of brushing your teeth. You don't decide to do it each night, weigh the pros and cons, or need to feel motivated. It just happens, because it always happens at that moment. That is what a habit is, and it is why habits are so useful for health: they keep working on the days when motivation doesn't.

## Cue, routine, reward

Habits are often described in three parts:

- **The cue**: the moment that sets it off. Waking up, sitting down to eat, switching off the light.
- **The routine**: the action itself. Daylight at the window, three breaths before the meal, a slow breath out in bed.
- **The reward**: what makes it worth repeating. Feeling more awake, a lighter stomach, a calmer head.

Habit research shows that the cue matters most. When you repeat the same action in the same situation, your brain starts to link the two. Over time, the situation alone is enough to start the action, without a decision. The reward helps at the start, while you are still choosing to do it. Later, the link carries it.

## Anchor the new to the old

The easiest cue is one you already have. You wake up every day. You eat every day. You go to bed every day. These are strong, reliable moments, and the track ties each habit to one of them:

- After you wake up: **Daylight first thing: head out of the window, and breathe**.
- When you sit down to eat: **Mindful eating: no phone, chew, breathe, a glass of water**.
- After the meal: **A 5-minute walk after eating**.
- In bed, lights off: **Optional: a mini breath before sleep**.

A useful way to set this up is a simple sentence: "After I [something I already do], I will [the new habit]." The clearer the moment, the less there is to remember.

## Why we start small

A small habit is easy to do on a bad day, and doing it on a bad day is what makes it stick. Studies of habit formation find that how consistently you repeat something, and how simple it is, both predict how strong the habit becomes. That is why the track starts with three minutes and not thirty. You can always do more once the habit is in place. It is much harder to keep something that felt like a chore from the first day.

## Missing a day doesn't break it

Habits take time. In one well-known study, people who repeated a simple daily health behaviour of their choice, at the same moment each day, felt it become automatic after an average of about 66 days, with large differences between people. A recent review found a similar picture: often around two months, sometimes much less, sometimes much more.

The same study found something reassuring: missing the occasional day did not seriously set people back. The habit kept building once they started again. So if you missed a day this week, don't start over in your head. Just pick it up at the next cue.

## What to do

No new habit today. Look at this week's habits and check each one has a clear cue:

- For each habit, name the moment it belongs to: waking, a meal, the afternoon, bedtime.
- If a habit keeps slipping, tie it to a moment you never miss.
- If it still feels like a chore, make it smaller.
- If you missed a day, do it at the next cue. No catching up needed.

## What to look for

Many people notice, as the days go on:

- A habit that starts before you have thought about it.
- That the cue begins to "remind" you on its own.
- That restarting after a missed day gets easier.

**Tie it to a moment you already have, keep it small, and pick it up again after a missed day.**
