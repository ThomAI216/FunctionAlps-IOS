The basics, done most days, do more for you than a perfect plan you can't keep.

You have reached the end of your first week. There is nothing new to add today. Today is for looking at what you have already done, and for one idea that sits under the whole track: simple actions, repeated, are what change how you feel.

## The trouble with the perfect plan

Most of us have started a perfect plan at some point. A new diet, a training programme, a morning routine with ten steps. It works well for a week or two. Then life happens: a late meeting, a sick child, a trip. The plan needs everything to go right, so it breaks the first time something goes wrong.

A plan like that also asks you to decide, every day, whether you have the time and the energy. Every one of those decisions is a chance to say "not today".

The habits in this track are built the other way round. They are small enough to fit into a bad day. Three minutes. A glass of water. Your head out of the window. That is not because small is all you can do. It is because small is what you can do every day.

## Your body reads signals, every day

Your body adapts to what you do often, not to what you do once. Morning light, the timing of your meals, a short walk, a slow breath, a regular bedtime: each one is a signal. One signal on its own does little. The same signal, day after day, is something your body can organise around.

This is the idea of the minimum effective dose: the smallest action that still sends a clear signal. A 5-minute walk after eating. Two minutes of stretching before bed. No caffeine after 16:00. None of these looks impressive. Repeated, they add up to more regular days, and regular days are where better energy, sleep and digestion tend to come from.

## Repetition makes it easier

There is a second reason to repeat the same simple things. The more often you do something in the same situation, the less you have to think about it. Research on habits describes this well: behaviour repeated in a stable context gradually becomes automatic, and automatic behaviour no longer depends on willpower or motivation on the day.

That is the quiet goal of these two weeks. Not to do more, but to make a few good things easy enough that they happen on their own. Tomorrow, in **Your day, on one page**, you will see how this week's habits fit together into one ordinary day.

## What to do

No new habit today. Use the time to look back at your week:

- Look at which habits happened on most days. Those are already becoming yours.
- Pick the one that felt easiest. Keep it exactly as it is.
- Pick the one that felt hardest. Make it smaller rather than dropping it: one minute instead of three, one good thing instead of three.
- If you missed some days, start again today. Nothing is lost.

## What to look for

Many people notice, by the end of the first week:

- A habit or two that now happens without a reminder.
- That some days feel more regular: waking, eating and going to bed at more similar times.
- That small things are easier to restart after a missed day than a big plan.

If you have a health condition and are unsure whether a habit suits you, ask your doctor or nutritionist.

**Simple actions, done most days, beat the perfect plan.**
