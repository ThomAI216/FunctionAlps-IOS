Long, unbroken sitting quietly slows your body down, and a 10-minute movement break every two hours is one of the easiest ways to undo it.

Think about a normal day. Breakfast sitting down, then the car, train or desk. Lunch at a table. More desk. The sofa in the evening. Many of us sit for most of our waking hours without ever deciding to. It doesn't feel like anything is happening, and that's the point: very little is.

## What long sitting does

When you sit for a long stretch, the big muscles in your legs and hips go almost silent. Those are the muscles that, when they work, help pull sugar and fats out of your blood after a meal. Switch them off for hours and the body handles food less efficiently. Blood flow in the legs slows down. Hips and hamstrings stay in one shortened position, and the upper back slowly rounds towards the screen.

Over years, the pattern adds up. A large review of more than a million people found that, even after accounting for exercise, those who sat for the longest each day had a higher risk of type 2 diabetes and of dying from heart disease. The risk seemed to rise most once total sitting went beyond roughly six to eight hours a day. These are observational studies, so they show an association, not proof that sitting alone is the cause. But the message is consistent: a workout in the morning is very good, and it doesn't fully cancel a day spent in a chair.

In the short term, most people feel it too: stiffness when they stand up, a heavy afternoon, a foggy head.

## Breaking it up is what helps

The good news is that you don't have to sit less overall to start feeling a difference. *Breaking up* your sitting matters on its own.

In controlled studies, people who interrupted long periods of sitting with short bouts of light activity had a smaller rise in blood sugar and insulin after meals than when they sat straight through. Light walking worked better than standing alone, and moderate movement worked a little better again. Most of these studies used breaks of a few minutes every 20 to 30 minutes. Our habit starts with a rhythm that is easier to keep, ten minutes every two hours. If you find you can stand up more often than that, even better.

Reminders help. In workplace studies, simple computer prompts reduced the number of long, unbroken sitting bouts. That is why the last step of today's habit is a quiet reminder: you shouldn't need willpower to remember.

## Moving through range

A movement break is also a chance to take your joints through the range that sitting takes away. You don't need a stretching routine. Stand up, reach your arms overhead, open your chest, rock gently into your hips, roll your shoulders, take a few slow steps or a few slow squats. The aim is not to force flexibility. It is to remind your hips, back and shoulders that they can move, under control and without pain, so they feel looser when you sit back down.

**Safety:** move slowly and never into pain. If you have a back, hip or knee problem, or a medical condition, check with your doctor or nutritionist about what suits you.

## What to do

**Move 10 minutes every 2 hours.** Every 2 hours, stand up and move for 10 minutes.

- **A walk if you can.** Around the block, down the corridor, to the kitchen and back the long way.
- **Stairs, a stretch, a call on your feet.** Climb a floor or two, open your chest and hips, or take your next call standing or walking.
- **Set a quiet reminder.** A gentle alarm every two hours on your phone or watch, so the break finds you.

## What to look for

Many people notice, often within a few days:

- **Less stiffness**, especially when you stand up after a long stretch.
- **Sharper focus after the break**, rather than a slow fade through the afternoon.
- **More steps without trying**, because the breaks add up across the day.

You may also see it in your evening check-in, in steadier energy across the afternoon.

**You don't have to give up your chair, just get out of it: ten minutes, every two hours.**
