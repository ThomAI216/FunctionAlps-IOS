Daylight first thing in the morning tells your body clock the day has started, and dimmer evenings let it wind down on time.

Most of us think of sleep as something that happens at night. But a good night often starts in the morning. The light you see in the first minutes after waking helps decide how awake you feel during the day, and how sleepy you feel at bedtime. It costs nothing, takes five minutes, and you can do it in your pyjamas.

## Your body runs on a clock

Inside your brain sits a small master clock. It keeps a rhythm of roughly 24 hours and sets the timing of many things: when you feel alert, when you feel sleepy, your body temperature, your hormones, even your appetite.

That clock is not perfectly accurate on its own. It needs a daily signal to stay in time with the outside world, and its strongest signal is light reaching your eyes. Light in the morning tells the clock "the day has started". Bright light late in the evening sends the opposite message, too late: it tells the clock the day is still going, and pushes your rhythm later.

So the pattern your body likes is simple: **bright days, dim evenings, dark nights**. An international group of light and sleep scientists put it in exactly those terms in their 2022 recommendations.

## Why outside, and why first thing

Indoor light is much weaker than daylight, even when a room feels bright to you. Outdoors, even on a grey Swiss morning, there is usually far more light than under a ceiling lamp. Window glass also takes some of it away. That is why the habit asks for fresh air: outside, on a balcony, or with the window open and your head out.

Timing matters too. Light early in your day is the light that helps your clock run on time. If you start the day in a dim room and go straight to your phone, your clock gets a weak, confusing signal.

The fresh air and a few slow breaths are part of it. They are a calm way to arrive in the day before messages, news and to-do lists arrive.

## The other half: screens at night

What you do in the evening matters as much as the morning. Bright screens close to your eyes in the last hours before bed can delay your clock. In one study, people who read on a light-emitting tablet before bed took longer to fall asleep, made less melatonin (the hormone that signals night), and felt less alert the next morning than when they read a printed book.

You don't need to live in the dark. Lower the lights in the evening, keep screens further away, and give your last hour to something calmer. The 3-2-1 rule, also part of today, covers this.

## What to do

The habit is **Daylight first thing: head out of the window, and breathe**.

- Right when you wake up, before your phone.
- Then 5 minutes outside, or at an open window.
- No sunglasses; never look straight at the sun.

You only need to be in the daylight, looking around as you normally would. Never stare at the sun, at any time of day. If your eyes are sensitive to light or you have an eye condition, check with your eye doctor first. In winter, when it is still dark when you wake, step out as soon as it gets light, or pair the habit with your first walk of the day.

## What to look for

Give it a week or two. Many people notice:

- They feel more awake in the morning, sooner.
- It is easier to fall asleep at night.
- Their mood feels steadier through the day.

Note your energy and sleep in your evening check-in, and see what your Trends show after a few weeks.

**Bright mornings and dim evenings are the simplest way to set your body clock: five minutes of daylight, before your phone, every day.**
