*The same few steps, in the same order, each evening tell your body that sleep is on its way.*

Think of how a child gets ready for bed: bath, pyjamas, story, lights out. Same steps, same order, most nights. It works for adults too. Your body cannot switch from a busy day to deep sleep in a minute. It needs a way down, and a routine is that way down.

Since day 4 you have been following the 3-2-1 rule. Today you add two steps, so your evening becomes a simple routine in three parts.

## Why the same steps help

Your body runs on a clock, and that clock likes regular signals. Light, food, activity and timing all tell it what time of day it is. When your evenings follow the same pattern, your body learns to expect sleep, and starts to get ready for it a little earlier: winding down, feeling drowsy at the right moment.

There is a second effect, in your mind. A routine you repeat becomes a cue. After a couple of weeks, simply opening your book or lying down to breathe starts to say "the day is over". You stop deciding what to do each evening, and decisions are one of the things that keep a busy mind awake.

To be honest about the evidence: each piece of good sleep habits has been linked with better sleep, but researchers have rarely tested a full routine as one package in the general public. So treat this as a sensible, low-risk experiment, and use your evening check-in to see how it works for you.

## Part 1: the 3-2-1 rule

You know this one (see *The 3-2-1 rule*). Three hours before bed, your last food of the day, so digestion is not working late. Two hours before, no hard exercise: a review of the research found that evening exercise does not usually harm sleep, but hard efforts ending within about an hour of bedtime can make it harder to fall asleep. Two hours gives a comfortable margin. Stretching, light movement and a walk are fine. One hour before, no scrolling.

## Part 2: a book instead of a screen

The last hour is where a book earns its place. In a careful sleep-lab study, people who read on a light-emitting tablet before bed took longer to fall asleep, made less melatonin (the hormone that helps signal night-time), had their body clock pushed later, and were less alert the next morning than when they read a printed book. Same reading, different light.

A book also gives your mind something calm to do. A story or a gentle subject pulls attention away from the day, without the pull of notifications.

## Part 3: 10 minutes to wind down

The last step is a deliberate slowing down, for about ten minutes. You have two options.

**Box breathing.** Breathe in for 4, hold for 4, breathe out for 4, hold for 4, and repeat. The even rhythm gives your mind one simple thing to follow. In a month-long randomised study, five minutes a day of structured breathing, box breathing among the options, improved mood. The version with long, slow breaths out did best, so if the holds feel tight, let your breath out be a little longer.

**An NSDR.** Non-sleep deep rest is a guided practice you do lying down, letting your body settle while you stay awake or half-awake. It has its own article: *NSDR, explained*.

## What to do

Your evening, in three steps.

**The 3-2-1 rule: 3 h no food, 2 h no intense exercise, 1 h no screens**
- 3 hours before bed: your last food of the day.
- 2 hours before: no hard exercise. Stretching, light movement and a walk are fine.
- 1 hour before: no scrolling. Answering a message is fine; it's the stimulating stuff you leave.

**A book instead of a screen**
- Choose tonight's book now.
- Paper, or an e-reader without the lights up.
- Even 10 pages.

**10 minutes to wind down: box breathing or NSDR**
- Box breathing: in 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4.
- Or a guided NSDR, lying down.
- Lights low.

Do the breathing sitting or lying down, never in water or while driving. If a hold feels uncomfortable, shorten it.

## What to look for

Many people notice:

- Falling asleep faster.
- Waking less in the night.
- Calmer evenings, with less noise in your head.
- Feeling sleepier at the right time.
- Better recovery, and a little more energy the next morning.

**A short routine, repeated every evening, teaches your body when it is time to sleep.**
