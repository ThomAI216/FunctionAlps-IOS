The habits that stay with you are the ones you would miss, not the ones you think you should do, so today you choose a few to keep.

Over the last twelve days you have tried a lot: meal photos, the evening check-in, walks after eating, morning movement, daylight, breathing, writing, a calmer evening. That was the point. Two weeks is a chance to taste each habit and see what it does for *you*. Today there is nothing new to add. Instead, you look back and decide what comes with you.

## Your body responds to what you repeat

Your body is always responding to signals: food, movement, light, sleep, stress, breathing. But it doesn't respond to what you *intend* to do. It responds to what you repeatedly expose it to. A walk once is pleasant. A walk most days, for months, is a signal your body can adapt to.

That is why the habits you keep matter more than the habits you tried. A short list you live by beats a long list you abandon in March.

## Two weeks is a start, not the finish

Habits take time to become automatic, the point where you do them without having to think. In a review of studies on health habits, the typical time was around two months, and it varied enormously from person to person: some people got there in days, others took most of a year. So if nothing feels automatic yet, nothing has gone wrong. You are early.

What helps a habit settle is simple: doing the same thing, in the same situation, again and again. The situation becomes the cue. Coffee in the kitchen, and your head goes out of the window. Lunch finished, and your feet head for the door. Lights low, and the book comes out. The more often the cue and the action meet, the less effort it takes.

## Keep the ones you'd miss

Here is the question we suggest you ask of each habit: *If I stopped this tomorrow, would I miss it?*

Not "is this good for me?" They all are. Not "should I be doing this?" That question keeps habits on a list, not in your life. The habits that last tend to be the ones that feel good while you do them, or the ones whose effect you can feel: the calmer evening, the lighter digestion, the clearer head after a walk. Research on exercise points the same way: how people feel *during* activity helps predict whether they keep doing it. And in the habit studies, habits that people chose for themselves, and habits done in the morning, tended to become stronger.

So be honest. If the breathing made your day, keep it. If the writing felt like homework, let it go for now, without guilt. You can come back to anything later.

## Small and anchored

Keep it to a few, the ones that earned their place. Give each one a fixed moment in your day, linked to something you already do. Keep the easy version: a five-minute walk you actually take is worth more than a twenty-minute walk you plan. And if you miss a day, start again the next. Missing once doesn't undo a habit; what builds it is repetition over weeks, not a perfect streak.

## What to do

- **Look back at your two weeks.** Open your Trends and see which curves moved, and when.
- **Ask of each habit: would I miss it?** Keep the ones where the honest answer is yes.
- **Choose a few to keep.** Let the others rest. They are not gone; you can bring any of them back later.
- **Anchor each one** to a moment you already have: waking up, a meal, the end of work, bedtime.

## What to look for

As you carry your chosen habits forward, many people notice:

- **Less effort** each week: the habit starts to happen on its own.
- **A day that feels like yours**, built on things you enjoy rather than rules.
- **Clearer signals** in your check-in, because you can see what each habit is doing.

You may also find you're more patient with yourself on the days you miss.

**Keep the habits you'd miss, anchor them to your day, and give them time: that is how they last.**
