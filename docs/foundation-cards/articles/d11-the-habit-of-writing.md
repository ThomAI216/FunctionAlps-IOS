*Five minutes of writing in the evening clears your head for sleep and, over the weeks, shows you your own patterns.*

Since day 5 you have been writing down three good things about your day. Today that habit grows a little. You still start with your three good things. Then you keep the pen moving for a few more minutes and write whatever is on your mind.

Why? Because the thoughts that keep you awake at night are often the ones you have not put anywhere. Writing gives them a place to go.

## Step one: three good things, still first

Three good things comes from positive psychology. In an early randomised study run online, writing down three things that went well each day, and why, for one week was one of the exercises that lifted happiness and eased low mood, with effects that lasted months after the week ended. Later research has been more mixed, and the effects tend to be modest. But the core idea holds: what you pay attention to grows. Spending a minute looking for the good things trains your attention to notice them (see *Three good things*).

Starting with the good also sets the tone. You go into the rest of your writing from a calmer, steadier place.

## Step two: what's on your mind

Then you write freely. A worry about work. Something someone said. A plan for tomorrow. A feeling you cannot quite name yet. There is no right topic.

The idea that writing about what is on your mind can help is one of the most studied in psychology. Psychologist James Pennebaker began testing it in the 1980s, asking people to write about their deepest thoughts and feelings in short sessions over a few days. A large review of 146 randomised studies later found a small but real benefit for health and well-being on average. Small is the honest word: writing is not a treatment, and it works better for some people than others. But it is free, it takes five minutes, and it has a long track record.

There is a practical benefit for sleep too. In a sleep-lab study, young adults who spent five minutes before bed writing a specific to-do list for the coming days fell asleep faster than those who wrote about tasks they had already done. Getting tomorrow out of your head and onto paper seems to let your mind let go of it.

## Clearing your mind, noticing patterns

Two things happen when you write regularly.

**Your mind clears.** A thought that goes round and round in your head often feels bigger than it is. Written down, it becomes one sentence on a page. You can see it, and often you can see what to do about it, or that it can wait until morning.

**Patterns appear.** You do not need to reread your notes for this. The act of writing makes you name things, and after a week or two you start to notice what keeps coming up: the meeting that always drains you, the walk that always helps, the evenings you sleep better. That is useful information, for you and for your work with us. Your evening check-in shows the same patterns in numbers; your writing shows them in your own words.

## What to do

Your habit from today: **5 minutes of writing: three good things, and what's on your mind**.

- Three good things first.
- Then whatever is on your mind.
- No rules, no rereading.

In your phone or a journal, whatever is easiest. Paper has one advantage: no screen in your last hour (see *Your evening routine*).

Writing can sometimes bring up difficult feelings for a short while; that is normal. If something heavy keeps coming back, please talk to someone you trust, to us or to your doctor.

## What to look for

Many people notice:

- A clearer head at night, with fewer thoughts going round in circles.
- Spotting what drains you and what helps, in your own words.
- Easier sleep, as tomorrow's to-dos are already on paper.

**Five minutes of writing turns a busy mind into a few lines on a page, and lets you sleep on a good thought.**
