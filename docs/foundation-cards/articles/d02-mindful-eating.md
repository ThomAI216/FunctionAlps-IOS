*Eating without your phone, chewing well and taking a few breaths changes how you digest, how full you feel and how much you want later.*

Think of your last lunch. Where were you? Many of us eat at the desk, in front of a screen, or standing up between two tasks. The meal is gone in a few minutes and we barely remember it. Then, an hour later, we are hungry again, or heavy and bloated.

Nutrition is not only what is on your plate. It is also how you eat it. That is where we start the nutrition pillar.

## Why the phone matters

When your attention is on a screen, your brain registers the meal less well. A review of experiments on attention and eating found that people who ate while distracted ate more at that meal, and even more at the next one. When people were helped to remember what they had eaten, they tended to eat less later.

In other words, a meal you didn't notice doesn't count as much for your brain. You finish it, but you don't really feel you've eaten. Putting the phone away is the simplest way to let the meal register.

## Why chewing matters

Digestion starts in your mouth. Chewing breaks food into smaller pieces and mixes it with saliva, which already begins to digest starches. Food that is chewed well is easier for your stomach and gut to handle.

Chewing also slows you down. Fullness signals take time to travel from your gut to your brain, and when you eat fast, you can finish before they arrive. A review of studies on chewing found that chewing more tended to reduce hunger and how much people ate, possibly through the gut hormones linked to fullness. The evidence is still early, but the direction is consistent.

A simple trick: put your fork down between bites. It gives you a natural pause and makes chewing well almost automatic.

## Why a few breaths, and a glass of water

Your nervous system has two broad modes: one for action and stress, one for rest and digestion. If you sit down to eat straight from a stressful meeting, your body is still in action mode. Three slow breaths before you start are a small signal to shift gear. Many people find that the meal feels calmer and sits better afterwards.

A glass of water with the meal is simply an easy way to drink enough through the day. Taking a sip between bites also slows the pace of the meal a little, which brings you back to chewing.

## Notice when you are full

Mindful eating is not a set of rules. It is paying attention: to the taste, to the texture, and to the moment you feel satisfied. Reviews of mindful eating suggest it works mainly by helping people listen to their internal signals of hunger and fullness, rather than to outside cues such as the size of the plate or the clock. The results are mixed across studies, but the approach is low-risk and many people find it helps.

Halfway through your meal, pause and ask: how hungry am I now? There is no right answer. You are just learning to hear your body again.

If you have, or have had, a difficult relationship with food, go gently, and talk to your nutritionist about what feels right for you.

## What to do

**Mindful eating: no phone, chew, breathe, a glass of water**
- Phone away, screen off.
- Chew each bite well; put the fork down between bites.
- Three breaths before you start, a glass of water with the meal.

Start with one meal a day. Lunch is often the easiest.

## What to look for

Over the next days, many people notice:

- lighter digestion, less bloating;
- feeling full sooner, with fewer cravings later;
- calmer meals.

**A meal you actually notice satisfies you more: phone away, chew slowly, breathe.**
